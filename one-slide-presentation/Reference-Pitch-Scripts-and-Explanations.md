# Reference Pitch Scripts and Explanations

These examples describe **ClearMail**, a fictional premium email service,
at three levels of achievement. Each script is approximately 120–130
words.

Aim to finish within **60 seconds**. A **15-second buffer** is available,
but you will be asked to stop at **75 seconds**.

The three-year subscription example comes from a real user's reported
experience. The proposed product, pricing strategy and expansion plans
are assumptions for this teaching case. Use genuine evidence in your own
pitch.

The explanations assess only:

- **Customer pain and value — 30 marks**
- **Commercial solution and business logic — 30 marks**
- **Scalability and growth potential — 20 marks**

---

## Example 1 — Outstanding

> "Hello, I'm Ronald. For professionals handling email all day, repeated
> clicking, rewriting and scheduling consume attention needed for their
> main work. One user has paid US$30 monthly for three years for a
> smoother email experience.
>
> ClearMail would combine keyboard shortcuts, AI-assisted replies and
> scheduling, and a calm interface across Mac and iPhone, working with
> existing email accounts.
>
> We would charge US$30 monthly, targeting people who value reduced
> friction enough to pay despite free alternatives. Our brand would
> emphasise dependable productivity, with no seasonal sales.
>
> We would reach similar users through professional referrals and team
> pilots. Shared integrations and standard onboarding could support more
> subscribers without custom builds.
>
> We would test time saved, renewal, and AI and support costs to establish
> whether growth remains profitable."

### Why this is outstanding

**Customer pain and value — 30 marks**

The script identifies a particular customer situation: professionals who
handle email repeatedly throughout their working day. Their pain is the
**time, attention and effort consumed by that workflow**.

This makes apparently simple functions commercially meaningful. A
shortcut may save only a small amount of effort on one occasion, but a
frequent user encounters the same action repeatedly. A calm interface
and consistent experience across devices may also reduce the effort of
managing daily communication.

The payment example is useful behavioural evidence. Someone has
continued paying US$30 monthly for three years—approximately US$1,080 at
that rate. This supports the proposition that at least one customer finds
sufficient continuing value to pay.

The limits still matter. One customer's experience does not establish
the size of the market, prove a particular amount of time saved, or
identify which feature caused continued renewal. It provides a credible
starting point for investigating similar customers.

**Commercial solution and business logic — 30 marks**

The features are connected to the customer's problem. Shortcuts reduce
repetitive actions; AI assistance supports replies and scheduling; the
interface and device continuity support a smoother workflow.

The payer and price are clear. The proposed customers are people who
place sufficient value on these improvements to pay US$30 monthly
despite free alternatives.

The commercial proposition is therefore more substantial than a list of
functions. It explains **why a customer might pay for improvements to an
activity they can already perform**. Differentiation could come from how
well familiar functions work together and how consistently the product
delivers the desired experience.

The proposed brand strategy also fits the chosen customer. Dependable
productivity and consistent premium pricing communicate whom the product
is designed for. However, avoiding seasonal sales does not itself prove
customer value or guarantee success. The strongest evidence would be
customers choosing the service and continuing to pay.

**Scalability and growth potential — 20 marks**

The script explains both **customer reach** and **repeatable delivery**.

Professional referrals could reach people with similar working habits.
Team pilots could introduce the service to several potential subscribers
through one workplace. These are plausible acquisition routes to test,
rather than claims that growth has already occurred.

Shared integrations and standard onboarding suggest that additional
subscribers could use the same underlying product without requiring a
separate custom build.

The script also recognises continuing costs. AI usage and customer
support may increase as subscribers are added. Testing these costs
alongside renewal would help determine whether recurring revenue
supports viable expansion.

This is a strong early scalability argument because it links a repeatable
customer need to reusable technology, identifiable acquisition channels
and costs that must remain manageable.

## Example 2 — Good

> "Hello, I'm Ronald. ClearMail is an email app for busy professionals. It
> would combine keyboard shortcuts, AI-assisted writing and scheduling,
> and a simple interface on Mac and iPhone.
>
> These features could help users handle messages faster and feel less
> overwhelmed. One user has paid US$30 monthly for three years for a
> comparable service, suggesting that some people will pay for a better
> email experience.
>
> We would charge US$30 per month and promote ClearMail as a premium
> productivity tool. We would advertise on productivity websites and
> encourage users to recommend it.
>
> We could expand internationally because the same app could serve users
> in different countries.
>
> Our next step would be to interview potential customers and compare
> their experience with free email tools before developing the product."

### Why this is good, with room for improvement

**Customer pain and value — 30 marks**

The script identifies an understandable customer benefit: handling
messages faster and feeling less overwhelmed. The continuing
subscription example supports the possibility of willingness to pay.

It also interprets that evidence carefully. "Some people will pay" is a
proportionate conclusion from one customer's experience.

However, "busy professionals" remains broad. The script does not
identify which users experience particularly costly friction, which
parts of their workflow cause difficulty, or how important the problem
is relative to their other needs.

A stronger argument would connect the product to a more specific
situation—for example, professionals repeatedly coordinating meetings
or processing substantial correspondence across devices. This would help
explain who is most likely to value the service enough to pay.

**Commercial solution and business logic — 30 marks**

The audience can understand the product, its functions and the
subscription model. The script offers a plausible connection between the
features and an improved email experience.

Its main limitation is the explanation of **why this particular product
deserves payment**. "A premium productivity tool" describes positioning,
but does not establish a distinctive advantage.

The proposed comparison with free tools is a sensible next step. That
comparison should identify the particular friction ClearMail would
remove and whether customers value the improvement sufficiently.

The US$30 price is informed by one comparable purchase, but that alone
does not establish an appropriate price for the proposed product or its
wider customer segment.

This is a good initial commercial answer because the product, payer and
revenue mechanism are clear. It needs a sharper explanation of customer
choice and value.

**Scalability and growth potential — 20 marks**

The script identifies plausible promotional channels and recognises that
the same software could serve customers in multiple countries. These are
useful beginnings.

However, it moves quickly from reusable software to international
expansion. It does not explain how additional users would connect their
accounts, learn the workflow or receive support without extensive
individual assistance.

It also leaves the economics unexplored. Advertising might attract
customers, but acquiring them must be affordable relative to what they
pay and how long they remain subscribers.

The next improvement would be to identify one practical growth mechanism
and test it. For example, a workplace pilot could examine whether a
standard onboarding process allows several colleagues to adopt the
product efficiently.

## Example 3 — Weak / Needs Work

> "Hello, I'm Ronald. ClearMail is a premium AI email app. Almost everyone
> uses email, so almost everyone could become our customer.
>
> We would offer keyboard shortcuts, AI writing and scheduling, and a
> beautiful interface on Mac and iPhone. These features would make us
> better than other email apps.
>
> One person has paid US$30 monthly for three years for something
> similar. This proves that millions of people would pay us the same
> price.
>
> We would never offer discounts, because that would automatically make
> our brand exclusive. Once enough people subscribe, the business would be
> profitable.
>
> Scaling would be easy because users worldwide could download the app. We
> would hire developers, launch internationally, and become the leading
> email service for busy people."

### Why this needs work

**Customer pain and value — 30 marks**

The script identifies email users, but using email does not establish a
significant problem or willingness to buy another tool.

Different users may have very different needs. Someone receiving
occasional personal messages may place little value on the same
improvements that matter to a professional handling correspondence
throughout the day.

The payment example is relevant, but the conclusion drawn from it is
unjustified. One customer's continued payment supports investigation of
similar customers; it does not prove that millions would pay.

The script needs to identify a specific customer segment, explain its
recurring difficulty and use evidence proportionately.

**Commercial solution and business logic — 30 marks**

The functions and subscription price are identifiable, so the script
deserves some credit for describing a product and revenue source.

However, it asserts that the functions make the product better without
explaining which customer problem they solve more effectively or why the
improvement justifies payment.

The branding argument is also incomplete. A high price and an absence of
discounts do not automatically create a valued premium brand. Customers
need a reason to regard the product as worth its price.

Likewise, enough subscribers do not automatically produce profitability.
Revenue must support development, infrastructure, AI usage, customer
acquisition and support. The script presents success as an expected
consequence of launching, without a credible commercial explanation.

**Scalability and growth potential — 20 marks**

Worldwide download availability establishes potential access. It does
not explain how suitable customers would discover the product, adopt it,
pay for it or continue using it.

The script also leaves repeatable delivery unexplained. More users could
require more onboarding and support, while AI usage could generate
continuing costs.

Hiring developers addresses initial product construction. It does not
establish an economical process for acquiring and serving additional
subscribers.

To strengthen this argument, the student should identify **one suitable
customer segment, one practical acquisition channel, and one repeatable
way to deliver the service at manageable cost**.
